# Findings — US Midterms Observatory 2026

Every number below is computed from the gold layer (`outputs/midterms.db`). Sources are named per table. 2026 figures are **preliminary** unless stated otherwise. The tone is neutral and explanatory: what happened, how, and why this cycle was different — evidenced by data.

## What happened

| Chamber | 2026 outcome | Net change vs 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| House | Democrats 254 seats, Republicans 162 (19 races uncalled) | Democrats +40, Republicans -56 |
| Senate | Democrats 53, Republicans 45, Independents 2 | Democrats +8, Republicans -8 |
| Governors | Democrats won 22 of 36 races, Republicans 12, 2 uncalled | Democrats +4 to +6 (seats after D 28–30, R 20–22) |

The 2026 midterms gave Democrats control of the House with 254 seats to the Republicans' 162, a net gain of 40 seats, with 19 races still uncalled. Democrats also took the Senate, moving from 45 seats to 53, a net gain of 8, and won 22 of 36 governor races. The House popular vote went to Democrats by 55.8% to 42.7%, a margin of +13.1 points. Compared with the previous three midterms, the scale stands out: in 2014 Republicans gained 13 House seats, in 2018 Democrats gained 41, and in 2022 Republicans gained 9 — the 2026 Democratic gain of 40 is the second-largest Democratic midterm gain in the nine-cycle series, after 2018, and the popular-vote margin is the widest in the series. All 2026 figures remain preliminary until the remaining races are called and certified.

## The comeback, measured

| Cycle | Net House change (president's party) | National House popular vote (D %) | D−R margin (pts) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2018 | Republican -42 | 53.40% | +8.60 |
| 2022 | Democratic -9 | 47.29% | -2.72 |
| 2026 | Republican -56 | 55.80% | +13.10 |

Measured against the two previous midterms, 2026 is a sharp reversal. In 2018 Democrats gained 41 House seats with 53.4% of the national House vote; in 2022 they lost 9 seats with 47.29%. In 2026 they gained 40 seats with 55.8% of the vote, a margin of +13.1 points over Republicans — the largest Democratic popular-vote margin in the 1994–2026 series. The Senate moved the same way: Democrats gained 8 seats in 2026 after losing the chamber in 2024. The state picture matches the national one: the largest Democratic House-seat gain by state was CA (+8 seats, 40 → 48), and only one state, ME, saw Democrats lose a net seat. Whether this is a durable realignment or a single-cycle swing cannot be answered from one election; the data only establishes the size of the 2026 move.

## The midterm pattern

| Cycle | President's party | Net House change |
|---|---|---|
| 1994 | Democratic | -54 |
| 1998 | Democratic | +5 |
| 2002 | Republican | +8 |
| 2006 | Republican | -30 |
| 2010 | Democratic | -64 |
| 2014 | Democratic | -13 |
| 2018 | Republican | -42 |
| 2022 | Democratic | -9 |
| 2026 | Republican | -56 |

The historical pattern is that the president's party loses House seats in a midterm. It held in seven of the nine midterms from 1994 to 2026. The two exceptions are 1998, when Democrats gained 5 seats under President Clinton, and 2002, when Republicans gained 8 under President George W. Bush. The losses range from 9 seats (Democrats, 2022) to 64 seats (Democrats, 2010). The 2026 result — Republicans losing 56 seats under President Trump — confirms the pattern, and is the second-largest loss in the series after 2010. The pattern is a description of what happened in these nine elections, not a law: two of nine cycles broke it, and the size of the loss varies from single digits to more than sixty.

Total candidate receipts for House and Senate races rose from $3,125,950,077 in 2018 to $4,139,563,155 in 2022, and $4,199,069,691 in 2026 — but the 2026 figure is a mid-cycle snapshot (filings through 2026-10-14) and will rise as post-election reports arrive, so the 2022-to-2026 comparison is not yet final. ## Geography

The state seat-swing table (Democratic House seats, 2022 → 2026) is in the scorecard. Three facts about it matter. First, the gains are concentrated: CA (+8) and New York (+5) account for 13 Democratic seats gained since 2022, with Iowa and Ohio each adding 3 and seven states adding 2 each; only one state, ME, saw Democrats lose a net seat. Second, the table covers 47 of 50 states — Alaska, Louisiana and North Carolina have no 2026 seat split in the source table, so no state-level net total is reported (the 47 known states sum to 248 Democratic seats, against a national infobox total of 254). Third, the seat changes partly reflect new maps, not only votes: California and Texas both redrew congressional maps mid-decade before the 2026 election, so a 2022-to-2026 seat comparison in those states mixes redistricting with vote shifts, and district-level swing figures would be misleading. The presidential geography runs the other way: between 2020 and 2024, the Democratic two-party presidential vote share fell in every state (mean state shift -2.19 points; largest falls in New York, New Jersey and Florida). County-level geography is not available: no public county-level vote data could be obtained, so no county flip count is reported.

## Split outcomes

In 2022, 7 states had a governor whose party differed from their House delegation's majority party. In 2026 the number is 4: Georgia (Governor Democratic, House delegation Republican); New Hampshire (Governor Republican, House delegation Democratic); Texas (Governor Democratic, House delegation Republican); Vermont (Governor Republican, House delegation Democratic). (Alaska and Kansas are excluded from the 2026 count because their governor races are uncalled, and states with evenly split delegations are excluded in both years.) The fall from 7 to 4 split states means fewer states split their ticket in 2026 than in 2022. Texas is the most notable new split: a Democratic governor with a Republican-majority House delegation (24 R, 14 D). Georgia is the other new split in 2026; New Hampshire and Vermont were split in both cycles.

## Texas deep-dive

| Office | 2014 | 2018 | 2020 | 2022 | 2024 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Governor (D margin, pts) | -20.4 | -13.3 | — | -10.9 | — | +7.1 |
| Senate (D margin, pts) | -28.4 | -2.6 | -9.9 | — | -8.7 | +12.1 |
| President (D margin, pts) | — | — | -5.7 | — | -13.9 | — |

Something changed in Texas, and the margins show it plainly. Republicans won the Texas governorship by 20.4 points in 2014, by 13.3 in 2018 and by 10.9 in 2022 — a steady narrowing — and in 2026 Democrat Gina Hinojosa beat incumbent Greg Abbott by 7.1 points (52.8% to 45.7%). The Senate moved further: Republican margins of 28.4 points (2014), 2.6 (2018), 9.9 (2020) and 8.7 (2024) became a Democratic win by 12.1 points in 2026, when James Talarico beat Ken Paxton 55.1% to 43.0% after Paxton beat incumbent John Cornyn in the Republican runoff. The Attorney General's office also flipped: Democrat Nathan Johnson beat Republican Mayes Middleton by 6.5 points (52.4% to 45.9%). That is all three statewide offices on the 2026 ballot going Democratic, breaking a Republican hold on every Texas statewide office that dates to the 1990s. The presidential picture is different: Texas voted Republican for president by 5.7 points in 2020 and by 13.9 points in 2024 — the state moved toward Republicans at presidential level while flipping Democratic in the 2026 statewide races. The Texas House delegation moved one seat toward Democrats (13 D / 25 R in 2022 to 14 D / 24 R in 2026), but that figure mixes the 2026 Texas redistricting with vote shifts and should not be read as a pure swing.
